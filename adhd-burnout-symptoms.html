What ADHD burnout feels like, and how it's different from normal tired

ADHD burnout is a tired that sleep doesn't fix. You can be exhausted and wired at the same time, and a weekend off barely touches it. Here are the signs I see most, and what helped me.

It's 5pm. You've held it together all day. Then someone drops a spoon and you lose it.

Later, you lie in bed so tired you could cry. And your brain won't switch off.

If that's your normal right now, you might be in ADHD burnout. A lot of ADHD folks are, and plenty don't know it has a name.

After one of my sessions, Clare F. wrote this to me:

"This was wonderful, made me realise I am actually burnt out... I didn't even realise it."

That's pretty common. You get so used to running on empty that it starts to feel like who you are.

ADHD burnout symptoms I see the most

ADHD burnout is what ADHD folks call a long stretch of running past empty. You won't find it in a medical textbook yet. These are the signs people tell me about, over and over:

  • You're tired all the time, and a good night's sleep doesn't change it.
  • You're wired and exhausted at once. The moment you stop, your body won't settle.
  • You swing between doing everything and crashing.
  • Small things tip you over. You're calm in a crisis, then undone by a Tuesday.
  • Even the things you love start to feel like a to do list.
  • Your head feels foggy and slow.
  • You look fine on the outside, so nobody checks on you.

You don't need all of them. Two or three, for weeks or months, is worth paying attention to.

How it's different from normal tired

Normal tired usually lifts with rest. You sleep in, you have a quiet weekend, and you feel more like yourself.

ADHD burnout tends to stay. You rest, and you're still tired. Sometimes resting even feels worse, because the second you stop, everything you've been pushing down catches up with you.

I see this in my own community too. Last summer, after the Christmas break, I asked people how they were going. Only 29 percent said they felt more rested and regulated. 43 percent said they were still burnt out, even after the holidays.

A break helps your diary. It doesn't always reach your body.

Why ADHD folks burn out

An ADHD brain works hard just to get through an ordinary day. Remembering. Starting things. Switching between things. Keeping track.

Then there's masking. Looking calm and on top of it while the inside is working flat out. I did that for most of my life, and I wrote about what ADHD masking looked like for me.

Add work, kids, parents who need you, money worries. Over 2,000 ADHD folks have done my burnout audit, and 486 of them said work was their biggest stressor.

None of that stops when you lie down. Your body stays on alert, stuck in fight or flight, waiting for the next thing. That's why rest on its own often doesn't work. I've written more about this in breathwork and ADHD.

What helped me

For me, things changed when I realised I needed to support my body and my nervous system to come out of fight or flight.

Breathwork was the thing that made the biggest difference. I felt different in my body after my very first session. Over a few months, the anxiety eased. I started to get a gap between something happening and how I reacted. Things that used to make me snap just didn't anymore.

The way I teach it now is gentle. You lie down, close your eyes and breathe for about 20 minutes or more. Then we talk about what came up.

That talk afterwards is the real key. Putting words to what came up helps it settle, and helps your brain file old stress in a new way. The breath on its own is one practice. The debrief is what makes it stick.

I still have ADHD. I still have hard weeks. But I'm not living past empty anymore. 💛

When to talk to your GP

Burnout can look a lot like depression, anxiety or a physical health problem. If you feel low most days, can't get out of bed, or have thoughts of harming yourself, please see your GP or a mental health professional. In Australia you can call Lifeline on 13 11 14, any time.

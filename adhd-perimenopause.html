ADHD and perimenopause: why everything got louder, and how breathwork can help

If your ADHD got louder in your forties, perimenopause may be part of why. Changing hormones can turn up the brain fog, the rage, the broken sleep and the anxiety. Here's what's going on, and where breathwork and mindfulness fit.

You spent years learning your ADHD brain. What sets you off. What you need on a bad day. How to get through.

Then one day, none of it works anymore.

You lose words in the middle of a sentence. You snap at the kids over nothing. You wake in the night and your brain switches straight on. The lists, the timers and the coffee stop helping.

A lot of ADHD folks tell me this happened to them in their forties. For many of them, it lines up with perimenopause.

What perimenopause does to ADHD

Perimenopause is the time before menopause, when your hormones start to change. It often starts in your early to mid forties, sometimes earlier, and it can last for several years.

Your hormones don't drop in a neat straight line. They go up and down. That swinging is a big part of why it feels so hard.

Oestrogen and dopamine are closely linked. Dopamine helps with focus, motivation and staying calm, and ADHD is closely tied to how your brain uses it. So when oestrogen dips, ADHD symptoms can get louder.

Then sleep gets broken up. And a tired ADHD brain has even less to work with the next day.

So this isn't you failing. Your body changed the settings underneath you.

What ADHD folks tell me is loudest

I asked the ADHD folks on my list what was loudest for them in perimenopause. The same four things came up again and again:

  • Rage that's out of your mouth before you can stop it, often at the people you love most.
  • Broken sleep. Waking in the night with your body exhausted and your brain wide awake.
  • Brain fog. Losing words, losing the plan, feeling less able than ever to organise your day.
  • Anxiety. The loop that won't let go.

And under almost every answer was the same thing. The tools that used to work for their ADHD stopped working.

One person told me their ADHD only really showed up in perimenopause. So they never got the chance to build tools for it at all.

Jo joined one of my programmes from the other side of the world. This is how she described life before she started:

"The coping strategies that I've built had become hard to keep up with and were just adding to the overwhelm."

Where I'm at with it

I'm on the edge of perimenopause myself.

For me it's the rage more than anything. I can feel it sitting closer to the surface at home than it used to. I rage, then I regret it, then I apologise. Around my period it's even worse. And my sleep is just starting to get broken up too.

I don't have it all worked out. But I do know what helps my body come back down, because I've been doing it for years.

How breathwork can help with ADHD in perimenopause

Let me be clear first. Breathwork won't change your hormones, and it isn't a treatment for perimenopause.

What it can help with is the state your nervous system is in while you go through it.

When you have less in the tank, your body tips into fight or flight faster, and takes longer to settle again. That's often what's behind the snap, and the night your body won't switch off.

The way I teach breathwork is gentle. You lie down, close your eyes and breathe for about 20 minutes or more while I guide you. Then we talk about what came up.

That talk afterwards is the real key. Putting words to what came up helps stress that's been stuck on loop start to shift.

For me, over a few months, I started to get a gap between something happening and how I reacted. That gap is exactly what rage takes away. Getting some of it back changed how I am at home.

After a few weeks of breathing with me, Jo wrote this:

"I feel like my capacity is coming back."

What about mindfulness and meditation?

Mindfulness means noticing what's happening right now, in your body and your mind, without rushing to fix it. That noticing really matters.

But a lot of ADHD folks tell me sitting meditation feels impossible. Sit still. Watch your breath. Bring your mind back every time it wanders. For an ADHD brain, that can turn into one more thing you feel you're failing at.

Nicola H. put it like this in her Google review:

"Unlike meditation, where I often wrestle with focusing on my breath, breathwork embraces the thoughts that arise and invites me to dive deeper."

Breathwork gives your busy brain something to do. You still practise noticing. I'll often ask, "How does that feel in your body?" You just don't have to force your mind to go quiet first.

I've written more about this in breathwork and ADHD, and about perimenopause anxiety and where breathwork fits.

If your ADHD has got louder lately, none of this means you're doing it wrong. It means you need support that fits the body you have now. For some people that's ongoing ADHD support with others who get it, like my ADHD community, The Rewire. 💛

Please talk to your GP too

If perimenopause is affecting your days, see your GP, ideally one who knows about menopause. There are medical options, including hormone therapy, that are worth a proper conversation. Breathwork sits alongside that support. It doesn't replace it.

If you feel low most days, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out now. In Australia you can call Lifeline on 13 11 14, any time.
